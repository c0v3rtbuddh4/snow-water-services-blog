This case study uses the three justice lenses (distributive, procedural and recognition) from Section 2. If you haven’t read it yet, it’s worth starting there.

Confession time: a couple of the images in this very blog were made with ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2026). Handy, right? But “the cloud” isn’t actually a cloud. It’s warehouse after warehouse of hot, humming servers, and many of them keep cool by evaporating water (Li et al., 2025). So every time we ask AI for a picture, a data centre somewhere takes a sip.

That sip is turning into a gulp. The International Energy Agency (2026) expects data centre electricity use to double by 2030. More computing means more heat, and more heat usually means more water. So, what happens when that thirst meets a city running dry? Let’s head to Uruguay.

When the taps turned salty

In 2023, Uruguay was hit by its worst drought in decades. By late June, Paso Severino, the larger of Montevideo’s two main reservoirs, was down to about 2.4% of its capacity (Doermann, 2023). To keep the taps running, the water utility started blending in brackish water from the Río de la Plata estuary. The allowable sodium limit was raised, and people who were pregnant or had high blood pressure or kidney disease were told to limit or avoid tap water (Doermann, 2023).

Residents described water that came out “saltier and saltier”, along with wrecked water heaters. One resident worked out that relying on bottled water would cost about a month’s salary (Associated Press, 2023).

Meanwhile, in the cloud…

At the same time, Google was planning a new data centre in Canelones, just outside Montevideo. The original design would have used up to 7.6 million litres of drinking water a day for cooling, roughly the daily water use of 55,000 Montevideo residents (Feliba, 2023).

The catch? Nobody was supposed to know. The water figures sat behind a confidentiality clause, and researcher Daniel Pena and his colleagues only got them released after taking the Ministry of Environment to court (Logic(s) Magazine, 2025). Google said the plans were still exploratory (Feliba, 2023), but the timing couldn’t have been worse. Protesters filled the streets with a slogan that says it all: “No es sequía, es saqueo”, or “It’s not drought, it’s pillage” (Logic(s) Magazine, 2025).

Sound familiar? It’s the Karragullen story from Section 2, but with Wi-Fi.

Running it through the justice lenses

Let’s bring back the three justice lenses from Section 2 (Menton et al., 2020):

  • Distributive: Households were rationing salty tap water and paying for bottles, while a multinational planned to cool its servers with drinking water inside a free trade zone (Yadav, 2024).
  • Procedural: The people sharing that water supply only found out what was planned because someone sued for the information.
  • Recognition: This one stings. In 2004, almost 65% of Uruguayan voters wrote access to drinking water and sanitation into their constitution as a fundamental human right, along with a rule that water be managed with citizen participation (Santos & Villareal, 2006). That’s six years before the UN got there (United Nations General Assembly, 2010). Yet when it came to the data centre, a confidentiality clause seemed to count for more.

Did pushing back work?

Kind of! After the backlash, Google shrank the project and switched to air cooling. Uruguay approved the redesign in 2024 (Yadav, 2024), and construction kicked off later that year, backed by more than US$850 million (Swinhoe, 2024). It’s not a free lunch, though: ditching evaporative cooling usually means using more electricity instead (Solomon, 2024).

And Uruguay isn’t a one-off. In Querétaro, Mexico’s booming “data centre valley”, some residents only get water a few days a week, while data centres in industrial parks are exempt from environmental impact reports (Baptista & McDonnell, 2026). Over in Tucson, Arizona, the city council unanimously rejected a massive data centre proposal, Project Blue, after weeks of public outrage over secrecy (Kunichoff, 2025).

What could a just and sustainable cloud look like?

The good news? The tools already exist.

  • Fit-for-purpose water: Remember from Section 2 that not every use needs drinking-quality water? Servers definitely don’t. Amazon already uses recycled water instead of drinking water at 20 data centre sites and plans to expand to more than 120 by 2030 (Swinhoe, 2025). Yes, treated wastewater could be cooling your chatbot.
  • Closed loops: Since August 2024, all of Microsoft’s new data centre designs use closed-loop, chip-level cooling that doesn’t evaporate any water for cooling (Solomon, 2024).
  • Transparency first: No secret deals. Publish site-level water use before approval and give locals a real say, which is exactly what SDG Target 6.b (community participation in water management) asks for (United Nations General Assembly, 2015).

So, what’s the lesson?

AI itself isn’t the villain. The real issue is who decides, and who goes without when the water runs short. Uruguay shows that a community pushing back can change how a multinational builds. As our digital lives grow, server farms are becoming part of the water services story, and they need to play by the same just and sustainable rules as everyone else.
