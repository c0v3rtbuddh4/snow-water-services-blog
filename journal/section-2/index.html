The Water Infrastructure Issues

A key part of providing water services is the infrastructure through which they are delivered. The United Nations defines water infrastructure as “a stock of facilities and installations needed to develop and manage water resources, including delivery, treatment, supply, and distribution of water to its users as well as for the collection, removal, treatment, and disposal of sewage and wastewater” (United Nations, 2006, p. 97). Financial, environmental and social challenges make water infrastructure one of the most difficult aspects of water services to manage across the globe. Let’s take a closer look.

Climate change is already reshaping the environment in ways most of us recognise, and it is quietly reshaping our water infrastructure too (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [IPCC], 2023). Firstly, more frequent and more intense heatwaves and droughts have led to higher peak water use and put strain on water supplies (LeChevallier, 2014). For regions already facing water stress and scarcity, such as Western Asia and Northern Africa, a lack of proper water infrastructure has devastating consequences for human lives and ecosystems (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations [FAO] & UN-Water, 2024). Climate trends that bring more runoff and salinity have reduced water quality, making water more expensive and difficult to treat (LeChevallier, 2014). More intense storms can cause power outages, which affect how water infrastructure functions, for example by limiting the ability to pump and treat water (LeChevallier, 2014). Changing rainfall patterns make it difficult to rely on large-scale rainwater harvesting as a water source, meaning new infrastructure must be built to source and treat water elsewhere (UN-Water, 2026a). Extreme rainfall can also destroy water infrastructure outright, as in Libya in 2023, where more than 11,000 people died after two dams collapsed during torrential rain (Ferdowsi et al., 2024). Overall, climate change has severely affected the world’s ability to source and deliver water of an acceptable quality, making it one of the most pressing challenges in the quest to achieve the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6: Clean Water and Sanitation.

Another challenge facing water infrastructure is maintenance and repair. Over time, water infrastructure degrades and becomes structurally unusable, as well as difficult and costly to maintain (Ferdowsi et al., 2024; Sowby et al., 2025). Much of today’s water infrastructure is decades old and was designed without considering the more extreme weather events that climate change brings (Ferdowsi et al., 2024). And here’s the major issue: replacing and repairing water infrastructure costs a lot of money. By a lot, we mean billions of dollars for each country (Sowby et al., 2025). In the USA alone, an estimated USD 129 billion is needed annually for the rehabilitation and replacement of water and wastewater infrastructure, yet only around 37% of this is actually funded (Sowby et al., 2025). Beyond the economic burden, there is also the problem of planning and carrying out construction and repair (Kerwin & Adey, 2020). Deciding what to build, and where to find the funding and labour, can be a lengthy process, especially when extensive and detailed infrastructure and water quality guidelines must be followed (Kerwin & Adey, 2020). Then there is the question of where to place infrastructure, and whether the works will cause service disruptions or traffic interruptions (Kerwin & Adey, 2020). Given the massive scale at which much of our water infrastructure operates, maintenance, construction and repair become even more complex.

So far, we’ve covered the environmental and economic challenges of running and maintaining water infrastructure, but what about the social issues? Two interlinked trends are central here: population growth and urbanisation. According to the UN, the world population is expected to grow to approximately 10.3 billion people by the mid-2080s (United Nations, 2026a). As mentioned before, water infrastructure already operates on a ginormous scale, so you can only imagine how much more will need to be planned, managed and maintained over the next 50 to 60 years. Urbanisation means that water resources will become more concentrated in major cities, making maintenance and repair even more difficult (Srinivasan et al., 2013). It also means more wastewater treatment facilities will need to be built to handle the additional waste. And growing populations mean greater demand for agriculture and energy, all of which requires (you guessed it) water (UN-Water, 2026b).

You get the point. Water infrastructure matters on every front: environmental, economic and social. Now that you know more about the infrastructure behind our water services, let’s look at what just and sustainable water practices actually involve, and the issues that come with them.

What Do “Just” and “Sustainable” Mean?

Can we really look after all of humanity while also protecting the ecological systems that keep the planet running? That’s the tension at the heart of “just and sustainable” water.

Economist Kate Raworth has a handy picture of this: the doughnut, the idea behind what is now known as “doughnut economics” (Doughnut Economics Action Lab, 2026). The inner ring is a social foundation: the basics everyone needs, like safe water, food and health. The outer ring is an ecological ceiling: the planetary limits we can’t push past without breaking things. The goal is to live in the doughnut itself, with nobody falling through the hole in the middle and nobody blowing through the crust.

Water sits right on both rings. Every person needs it, and every river, wetland and aquifer does too. So, what happens when those needs collide?

First, though, we need to define what “just” and “sustainable” actually mean.

Sustainable is about protecting the environment and conserving resources so they’re still there for future generations. It used to be treated as a purely “natural environment” idea, but today it stretches to include the social, economic and political systems we live in too. For now, though, let’s focus on the environmental component.

Just is about fairness: equity, fair access and human rights. In 2010, the UN formally recognised access to safe water and sanitation as a human right (United Nations General Assembly, 2010). Environmental justice researchers usually break fairness into three parts, which make a useful lens for everything that follows (Menton et al., 2020):

  • Distributive justice: who gets the water, and who ends up with the pollution and the bill?
  • Procedural justice: who gets a seat at the table when decisions are made?
  • Recognition justice: whose values, knowledge and rights are taken seriously in the first place?

These questions aren’t unique to water, but water makes them painfully concrete, because you can’t opt out of needing it. Spoiler: humans are very good at making this complicated.

Tension 1: Conservation vs access

To protect rivers and aquifers, governments set extraction caps, licences and environmental flow rules. That’s the “sustainable” side of the ledger. The catch is that these rules only work if they cover who is actually taking the water, and if the price of taking it reflects what it’s worth. A close-to-home example shows how these measures can slip.

Bottled water: seems harmless, right?

In Karragullen, in the Perth Hills, water trucks have been pulling up at a private rural property for decades to cart groundwater away for Coca-Cola’s bottled water. The City of Armadale first approved the extraction in 1992 (Weber, 2024).

The kicker? The water was free. The bore sits in an “unproclaimed groundwater area”, so under WA rules no licence was needed and no fees applied. It was all perfectly legal.

Then Perth had its driest stretch on record. In 2024, neighbouring orchardists and residents went public: some said their own bores were struggling, and nobody was really monitoring how many trucks were leaving. After public outcry, the Water Minister said the arrangement didn’t pass the “pub test” (Dietsch, 2024). Coca-Cola voluntarily paused extraction to let the aquifer recover, and the government reviewed the arrangement (Reynolds, 2024).

That’s distributive justice in a nutshell: a multinational was taking a shared, shrinking resource for free, while locals were paying to truck in their own water. Ouch!

Tension 2: Who pays, and who profits?

Someone has to pay for pipes, treatment plants, and upgrades. The question is who, and whether the money actually goes back into the system. For a cautionary tale, look at England.

England: what a royal mess

England privatised its water and sewerage authorities in 1989, and they were sold with no debt (Laville, 2023). By 2023, the companies had racked up more than £64 billion in debt while paying out more than £78 billion in dividends. Critics argue that money should have gone into infrastructure for a growing population (Mitchell, 2026).

The results are hard to miss. In 2024, English water companies discharged untreated sewage into rivers and seas over 450,000 times, for a combined 3.6 million hours (The Rivers Trust, 2025). Storm overflows are supposed to be emergency valves, not business as usual. Meanwhile, customers are paying more: bills in England rose by about 26% on average in 2025–26 (Water UK, 2026).

This hits every kind of justice at once. Households pay more, rivers get worse, and the benefits flow to shareholders who may not live anywhere near the river.

What about here?

In WA, the main water utility, Water Corporation, is state-owned, and most Australian urban water utilities are publicly owned. That doesn’t make them perfect, but it does mean profits aren’t being funnelled offshore. England is a good reminder of what’s at stake whenever “just let the market handle it” gets floated as the answer.

Tension 3: Whose rights count?

Unfortunately, despite our sunny reputation, Australia isn’t all rainbows and sunshine. For First Nations peoples, water isn’t just an economic or environmental asset. It’s tied to Country, culture, identity and responsibilities that go back tens of thousands of years. Yet Australian water law has mostly treated water as something to be allocated, traded and priced, with little room for ancestral stewardship.

Who owns the water?

The Murray–Darling Basin is the clearest example. Over 50 First Nations have deep connections to its rivers. Yet First Nations people own less than 0.2% of the Basin’s surface water entitlements, and just 0.02% of its groundwater entitlements. For comparison, around 5.3% of the Basin’s population is Indigenous (Hartwig et al., 2021).

First Nations organisations have long called for water entitlements legally owned by Indigenous Nations, in sufficient quantity and quality to support their cultural, spiritual, environmental, social and economic wellbeing. There’s been progress: the federal government’s $100 million Aboriginal Water Entitlements Program made its first purchase, of 200 megalitres, in 2025 (Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water [DCCEEW], 2026). But cultural flows still haven’t actually been granted for the Basin’s river system (Brown & Kennedy, 2023).

This is recognition justice. Even well-meaning “sustainable” water plans can sideline Indigenous rights if they only see water as a number in an allocation table.

What comes out of the tap?

Then there’s the water people actually drink. In the two years to 2019–20, testing in WA’s remote Aboriginal communities found E. coli or Naegleria in 21 communities, high nitrate in 19 and high uranium in 4, according to the WA Auditor General (Office of the Auditor General Western Australia, 2021). Some communities have had to rely on bottled water (Bamford & Parke, 2021).

Think about that next to our bottled water story. In one part of WA, groundwater is trucked away for free and sold in bottles. In another, bottles are shipped in because the local water isn’t safe to drink. That’s the “just” part of the equation failing within our own home state.

There’s no tick-the-box solution here. “Just” and “sustainable” will always pull against each other a little, and managing that tension, rather than wishing it away, is what the rest of this blog is about.

What If We Get the Balance Right?

If “just” and “sustainable” will always pull against each other a little, the real question becomes what happens when we get the balance right. Between ageing pipes, climate pressures and the injustices we’ve just covered, it can all feel a bit bleak. So, let’s flip it. If just and sustainable practices were fully applied today, not just as buzzwords in strategy documents and press conferences, what would water services look like? And, more importantly, what good would they do?

What “just” looks like when it’s done properly

Earlier, we set out three lenses for fairness (Menton et al., 2020). Here’s what each looks like when it actually works, with the human right to water and sanitation treated as the baseline, not an aspiration (United Nations General Assembly, 2010).

Distributive justice done properly means the quality of water from your tap, or the reliability of your wastewater removal, doesn’t depend on your postcode. Unfortunately, the remote WA communities mentioned earlier aren’t outliers. Nationally, at least 25,245 people in regional and remote locations weren’t supplied with water that met health-based guidelines (Wyrwoll et al., 2022). And this isn’t an old study, either: these figures are from 2018–19. When larger towns (more than 1,000 people) are included, the figure rises to nearly 200,000 people receiving unsuitable water, and monitoring gaps mean the real number could well be higher (Wyrwoll et al., 2022). A just system closes that gap, funding regional services to the same standard as the capitals, so that everyone gets the same access to safe and reliable water services, no matter their location, income or employment.

Procedural justice would require communities to be engaged in decisions about their own water services, from where a new treatment plant goes, to how tariffs are set, to what quality standards they expect. Think back to Karragullen, where locals only got a voice after going public. Had the process been just, they’d have had a seat at the table from day one.

Recognition justice goes a step further, requiring that all knowledge, values and cultural connections to water are treated as valued and legitimate inputs to planning and design. In the Murray–Darling, that would mean flows for cultural purposes being granted rather than sitting in purgatory. Put together, these three turn water services from something done for people into something done with them, improving outcomes for everyone.

What “sustainable” looks like when it’s done properly

On the sustainability side, the biggest and most impactful shift is from treating wastewater as something to be discarded to treating it as a resource that helps people and the environment. Every litre of effluent discharged to the ocean takes its nutrients with it, plus the energy we spent treating and pumping it. We pay to supply it, pay to clean it, then throw it away. What a waste! And as urbanisation and populations grow, that untapped resource only gets bigger.

The principle behind this shift from waste to resource is “fit-for-purpose” water. Fundamentally, this means treating recycled water to the quality its end use actually needs, and managing the associated risks (Natural Resource Management Ministerial Council et al., 2006). Irrigating a sports oval or vineyard doesn’t need the same quality or aesthetics as drinking water, so why should it need the same treatment, cost or energy input?

South Australia’s Virginia Pipeline Scheme shows this in practice. Since 1997, it has delivered around 20 GL of recycled water a year to market gardens and food producers (SA Water, 2026). Before the scheme and the Bolivar treatment plant were built, growers were drawing about 18 GL a year from aquifers that studies showed could only sustainably supply 6 GL, and as a result, large volumes of seawater were being drawn into the region’s groundwater (Primary Industries and Regions SA, 2026). This shows how sustainable practices like recycling can tackle a supply problem and an environmental problem at the same time.

At the other end of the scale, Windhoek, Namibia’s capital, has been treating municipal wastewater to drinking standard and feeding it directly into the city’s supply since 1968 (Onyango et al., 2014). Unlike in most developed countries, residents broadly accept it and are proud to be leading the world in direct potable reuse (Onyango et al., 2014). That matters massively, because public stigma and misinformation are among the biggest barriers to truly sustainable practices right now. The technology has advanced; people just take more time.

Why it matters when both line up

This is where it gets interesting. Get both right and we’re living in the doughnut from earlier: nobody falling through the hole, and nobody blowing through the crust. Recycled water gives farmers a climate-independent supply through droughts and heatwaves, and leaves more water in rivers and aquifers. Safe drinking water in every community cuts disease and its costs. And those eye-watering infrastructure bills aren’t just a cost: a global analysis found that every dollar invested in water and sanitation returns about US$4.30 in benefits, counting only health and time savings (Hutton, 2013). Justice also makes sustainability stick, because people are far more likely to trust recycled water when they’ve had a real say in the scheme.

This is also why SDG 6 doesn’t sit on its own: progress on clean water and sanitation feeds directly into SDG 3 (good health), SDG 11 (sustainable cities) and SDG 13 (climate action) (United Nations General Assembly, 2015).

None of this is science fiction. Most of the technology already exists, and some has been running for over half a century. What has really changed is how we think about water: from something we take, use and dispose of, to something we share and keep in the loop. The next step is to put these ideas to the test in the real world.
