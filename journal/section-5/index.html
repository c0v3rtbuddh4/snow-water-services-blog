This final section brings together the issues from Section 2 and the lessons from both case studies.

From Paper to Practice

A sustainable water future can look convincing on paper. New infrastructure can improve water security, reduce environmental pressure and allow for better community-based services. But there is an important, and often difficult, link between developing a proposed solution and making it part of daily life. Before any new scheme becomes reality, some tricky questions need answering:

  • Who will pay for the new infrastructure?
  • Who has the authority to decide where it should be located?
  • Who will maintain it?
  • What if the interests of different local communities or jurisdictions are at odds with each other?

These questions matter because, while the technology behind water services can work well, a water service can’t be judged on its technical capabilities alone. Its long-term success depends on many factors, including financial feasibility, governance and fairness. So the next generation of challenges won’t be about finding the “one best” solution.

Infrastructure costs

Cost is a major barrier to new water infrastructure. While it is relatively easy to estimate the cost of building something new, the total cost of an asset extends well beyond construction to include operating costs (such as energy), maintenance and replacement parts, and eventual replacement. These ongoing expenses make new infrastructure even more challenging to deliver. For example, securing adequate financing for the construction, operation and maintenance of green and blue infrastructure has been a major obstacle to scaling up China’s Sponge City Program (Chan et al., 2022).

Sustainable design challenges

On top of this, reviews have shown that many environmental externalities and wider benefits of urban nature-based solutions are rarely fully quantified, because they are difficult to put a monetary value on (Chelli et al., 2025). So, instead of asking “which option is the cheapest?”, we need to ask “which option delivers the greatest long-term benefit for each dollar spent?”

There is also a justice element to these comparisons. Ultimately, someone pays for all infrastructure, either directly through taxes or indirectly through higher utility bills, and funding may also come from government grants or private investment. This creates a key issue for sustainable design. Nature-based water systems can look costly in terms of capital expenditure, yet the value of the many benefits they provide is often difficult to quantify using traditional financial assessments, and those benefits are likely to flow through to the whole community. When evaluating new water projects, we should therefore consider both financial sustainability and distributive justice: as well as keeping the system affordable, we must make sure its costs and benefits are shared fairly across the community.

More than just numbers

Funding alone doesn’t guarantee that infrastructure gets built and used successfully. Even when there is enough money, governments and water utilities set construction priorities, create the regulatory frameworks that projects must work within, and decide which projects go ahead. In other words, politics plays a big role in shaping the type and quality of water services that communities will experience for decades to come.

Research comparing sustainable stormwater management in urban areas of China and Sweden shows this clearly. In both countries, governance structures had a strong influence on how sustainable stormwater practices were implemented. National policies and plans played a key role in China, whereas local political factors, including public awareness and trust in system performance, were critical in Sweden. Funding for long-term maintenance was a challenge in both (Qiao et al., 2019).

As noted earlier, water infrastructure is a long-term commitment. It typically takes many years before a project can begin construction, and once built, it must be operated and maintained for decades. Over this time, political priorities, budgets and community expectations can change significantly. To design sustainable water services that thrive despite these shifts, we need long-lasting governance arrangements that keep a project functioning without interruption throughout its lifespan.

Community participation is an integral part of developing new sustainable water services. Even if a project is technically feasible, there’s no guarantee residents will support it if they don’t understand how it works, or feel they had no input into the decision. Research on urban water nature-based solutions in Sydney found that residents place ecological, recreational and functional value on these ecosystems, and that social and cultural dimensions are important for achieving more equitable governance (Naserisafavi et al., 2022).

To achieve fairer governance, then, public engagement needs to happen before a final decision is made, not after, and should continue while alternatives are still being considered.

Water becomes geopolitical

All of these questions become far more complex when a water resource crosses political borders. Rivers don’t recognise international boundaries, and decisions made by one nation don’t necessarily affect only that nation. In fact, they can sometimes harm the water security of its neighbours.

For example, China, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam share the Lancang–Mekong River Basin, which supports many different water uses. Transboundary cooperation has been shown to potentially increase benefits for stakeholders, especially during droughts. Yet Li et al. (2019) note that cooperation also raises questions about how the additional benefits it generates should be shared among stakeholders.

The lesson for future water planning is an important one: when water resources are shared, no single nation can achieve water security on its own. Sustainable management of shared water resources requires coordination between nations, including sharing data and information, negotiating agreements and working together (Li et al., 2019).

Access to finance, technology and energy

Finally, geopolitics can also affect how water infrastructure is actually delivered, by shaping access to finance, technology and energy, especially as cities increasingly adopt highly sophisticated treatment and water management systems. Future water strategies must therefore consider not only what can be built locally, but also the outside relationships and resources needed to make it happen.

The connection

All of these issues are interconnected. A city may find an innovative technical solution, but that solution only comes to life with funding. So, what determines the funding, and how much is available? That comes down to governance and political priorities, which in turn shape where infrastructure is built, who benefits and who bears the costs. And when shared water resources, or the components needed to use them, cross national borders, cooperation becomes essential too.

So, before declaring any new water system sustainable and just, we should test it against five criteria:

  1. Can it operate effectively?
  2. Can it be funded and maintained?
  3. Was everyone engaged in the decision?
  4. How are the benefits and costs distributed?
  • Do its impacts reach beyond its own users, community or jurisdiction?

Applying these criteria gives us a clearer picture of how to measure the success of future water systems. They also let us compare two very different visions of future water systems, as presented in the advertisement in our . The advertised vision depicts a future built on environmentally friendly technological innovation, but we know that truly sustainable systems depend on many other decisions too: how those innovations are funded and governed, who takes part in decisions, and how the benefits and costs are shared across society.

We can’t expect any single technological innovation to solve every water challenge. A more realistic goal is to develop technologies and manage systems case by case, suited to local conditions and with full consideration of their economic, social and environmental implications. Sustainability may start as an engineering problem, but delivering it depends just as much on governance, economics and cooperation.

For the Betterment of Everyone

This brings us to where the blog has been heading all along: the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). They’re the yardstick everything else gets measured against, and Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation for everyone by 2030) is the one that ties our entire story together.

Here’s the uncomfortable part: we’re not on track to meet this goal. In 2024, around 2.1 billion people still lacked safely managed drinking water, and 3.4 billion lacked safely managed sanitation (World Health Organization [WHO] & United Nations Children’s Fund [UNICEF], 2025). To close that gap by 2030, the world needs to move roughly eight times faster on drinking water than it currently is (UN-Water, 2026). The ageing, underfunded pipes we looked at in Section 2 are a global story too, so doing more of the same isn’t going to cut it.

What makes Goal 6 worth the effort is how much else relies on it. UN-Water (2026) describes water as a superconnector across the 17 goals. Unsafe water and sanitation were linked to roughly 1.4 million deaths in 2019 (Wolf et al., 2023), which ties directly to Goal 3 (good health). Women and girls still spend about 250 million hours a day just collecting water (United Nations, 2026), a burden directly linked to Goal 5 (gender equality). And because climate is the force stressing every system we’ve described, Goals 6 and 13 rise or fall together. The good news is that how we get there isn’t a mystery. It’s everything this blog has already covered. Perth’s GWRS is Target 6.3 in action, turning wastewater into a resource. Fit-for-purpose recycling is Target 6.4. The basin-wide planning we made the case for is Target 6.5, though at current rates the world won’t achieve it until about 2049 (United Nations, 2025). That proves the point that the real bottleneck is governance and funding, not technology. Bringing communities in early, as Perth’s trial did, is Target 6.b. Just and sustainable may pull against each other at times, but they aren’t two separate jobs. They’re one job, done properly.

Australia shows exactly why the “just” part matters. On paper, we’re brilliant, with over 99% of us having access to safely managed water (WHO & UNICEF, 2025). But that average hides two things. The first we saw in Section 2: 25,245 people in small communities received water that failed to meet health guidelines (Wyrwoll et al., 2022). The second is that First Nations peoples hold under 0.2% of the Murray–Darling’s surface water (Hartwig et al., 2021). The new National Water Agreement finally names First Nations water interests as a core objective, but as of early 2026, only the federal government had signed it (Sorbello & Larsen, 2026). Recognition on paper and water in the tap are still two very different things.

None of this is hopeless. Between 2015 and 2024, 961 million more people gained safely managed water (WHO & UNICEF, 2025), so change at scale clearly works. And the moral case was settled back in 2010, when the UN recognised water and sanitation as a human right (United Nations General Assembly, 2010), a vote from which Australia, surprisingly, abstained.

That’s what the betterment of everyone really means: not just more water, but water that reaches the remote community as reliably as it reaches the capital city. Get the balance of just and sustainable right, and we land back inside the doughnut, with nobody falling through the floor and nobody breaking through the ceiling. We already have the tools. What’s missing is the will to use them for everyone.
