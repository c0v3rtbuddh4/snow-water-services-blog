Like Case Study 1, this case study draws on the three justice lenses from Section 2.

We’ve seen what can go wrong when water is taken quietly. But what does it look like when a whole city’s wastewater is put back to work?

Perth’s Groundwater Replenishment Scheme (GWRS) is a great example. It shows how we can turn wastewater into a valuable resource, but it also raises an important question: just because a solution is environmentally sustainable, does that automatically make it fair?

Turning wastewater into a resource

As we saw in the introduction, declining rainfall has made Perth’s dams less reliable, so the city now relies on a mix of groundwater, desalination, groundwater replenishment and dam water (Water Corporation, 2026b).

So, why not reuse the water we already have? That’s exactly what the GWRS does.

Located in Craigie, the scheme takes treated wastewater from the Beenyup Water Resource Recovery Facility and purifies it through advanced treatment, including ultrafiltration, reverse osmosis and ultraviolet disinfection. The purified water is then injected into deep underground aquifers, where it is stored before being recovered and treated again for drinking-water supply (Department of Health, Western Australia, 2026; Water Corporation, 2026a).

The scheme began full-scale recharge in 2017. In 2022, Stage Two doubled its capacity from 14 to 28 billion litres per year, following an investment of approximately $320 million (Water Corporation, 2022).

By March 2025, the scheme had recharged more than 100 billion litres of purified recycled water into Perth’s aquifers (Water Corporation, 2025). That’s a lot of water being put back into our water system instead of simply being discharged!

Sustainable? Absolutely. But is it just?

From an environmental perspective, the benefits are clear. Instead of simply disposing of treated wastewater, Perth can reuse it to support future water supplies. This is a practical example of the shift from waste to resource discussed in Section 2. But sustainability is only half the story.

Remember the three types of justice from Section 2: distributive, procedural and recognition justice? Let’s see how they apply to Perth.

First, who benefits?

Unlike the Uruguayan data centre, which would have served a single business, groundwater replenishment contributes to Perth’s wider public water supply. This can help improve water security across the city, particularly as rainfall becomes less reliable.

But here’s another question: who pays?

Stage Two alone cost approximately $320 million, including a second advanced water recycling plant, recharge and monitoring bores, and a 13-kilometre transfer pipeline (Water Corporation, 2022).

Advanced treatment, pumping and monitoring also require ongoing investment. If these costs are eventually reflected in household water bills, affordability could become a concern, particularly for lower-income households. So, although the scheme supports sustainability, we still need to consider whether its costs and benefits are shared fairly.

Second, do people have a say?

Imagine being told that your future drinking water could originally come from wastewater. Would you feel comfortable drinking it? Even when recycled water meets strict drinking-water standards, some people may still have concerns about its safety.

Interestingly, Perth’s original groundwater replenishment trial considered community engagement and acceptance alongside technical feasibility and government regulation (Water Corporation, 2026a). For us, this shows that good engineering isn’t just about making technology work. It’s also about helping people understand and trust the systems they depend on.

Third, whose concerns are recognised?

Recognition justice reminds us that different communities may have different values, experiences and concerns about water. For future water projects, we think these perspectives should be respected, rather than assuming that technical approval alone is enough. A project can be environmentally sustainable, but it also needs public trust and fair decision-making to succeed.

What about environmental costs?

Of course, groundwater replenishment isn’t perfect. Advanced treatment requires energy, infrastructure and continuous monitoring, so although recycling wastewater can reduce pressure on conventional water sources, it still has environmental and financial costs.

There is, however, an interesting advantage. According to Water Corporation (2022), groundwater replenishment uses around a quarter of the energy required for seawater desalination, because treating wastewater generally requires less pressure than removing salt from seawater. This suggests that recycling wastewater can be a more energy-efficient option within Perth’s water supply system.

Still, we don’t think the goal should be to find one perfect solution. Perth already relies on several water sources, including desalination, groundwater, groundwater replenishment and rainfall runoff into dams (Water Corporation, 2026b). Combining these sources with water conservation and better demand management may provide a more reliable water future than depending on any one technology alone.

What can Perth teach us about the future?

For us, Perth’s GWRS demonstrates something important: a technically successful water project is not automatically a completely just one. We should look beyond how much water a system can produce, and ask who benefits, who pays and who gets involved in decisions.

Perth shows that wastewater can become a valuable resource rather than something we simply throw away. But achieving a truly sustainable and just water future takes more than advanced technology. It also takes affordability, environmental responsibility and public trust.

And that brings us to the next challenge: even when we know what a better water system looks like, how do we actually pay for it, manage it and make it work in the real world?
